Training Quantum Neural Networks with TensorFlow Quantum: a Small, Reproducible Study

Amal Mehta, independent researcher2026PDF version
Abstract

Quantum neural networks (QNNs) are easy to describe and surprisingly hard to evaluate fairly. We present a small, fully reproducible study built on TensorFlow Quantum, aimed at readers who want to see the main behaviours of QNN training first-hand. A 17-qubit Farhi–Neven QNN learns to separate 3s from 6s in $4\times4$ binarised MNIST, reaching 0.868 test accuracy over 3 seeds. A classical network with 37 weights reaches 0.909, and adding an 8-unit classical head to the same circuit lifts it to 0.906. Both are close to the 0.916 that a lookup table over the training patterns sets as a ceiling for this input. Gradient variance in random circuits falls 27-fold from 2 to 8 qubits. Under depolarizing noise, models trained without noise drop to 0.770 at $p=0.01$ and to chance at $p=0.03$. Code, raw results and the scripts that generate every number in this paper are released.

1 Introduction

Parameterised quantum circuits trained by gradient descent are the most common model in near-term quantum machine learning [11, 3]. Software such as TensorFlow Quantum (TFQ) [2] lets anyone train them on a laptop. That makes it easy to run a demo, and also easy to misread one: without a matched classical baseline, a sense of what the data allows, or a look at gradients and noise, a working QNN says little.

This paper is a compact, reproducible tour of those checks on one task: the Farhi–Neven QNN [4] on $4\times4$ MNIST 3-vs-6, the setting of the TFQ tutorial. We contribute:

  • a matched comparison of the QNN, a classical network of similar size, and a hybrid model that adds a classical head to the same circuit, alongside the accuracy ceiling the input allows (§4.1, §4.2);
  • measurements of gradient variance in random circuits as width and depth grow (§4.3);
  • the effect of depolarizing noise, both on trained models and during training (§4.4);
  • code that regenerates every figure, table and number from raw results with one command, running on current TFQ (0.7.6) in Docker.

None of these phenomena are new. The point is to reproduce them together, with error bars, in a form that is easy to rerun and extend.

2 Background

Quantum neural networks. A parameterised quantum circuit (PQC) applies gates whose angles are trainable, and its output is the expectation value of an observable. Trained by gradient descent, PQCs are the near-term analogue of neural networks [11, 3]. Farhi and Neven [4] proposed a QNN for binary classification in which every data qubit couples to a single readout qubit, and the prediction is $\langle Z\rangle$ on that readout. We use their architecture throughout.

Gradients. On hardware, gradients come from the parameter-shift rule, which evaluates the circuit at shifted angles [10, 12]. In exact classical simulation, the adjoint method gives all gradients at roughly the cost of one forward and one backward pass [6].

Barren plateaus. For sufficiently random circuits, the variance of the gradient with respect to any parameter decays exponentially in the number of qubits [9]. Hardware noise can induce the same effect even in shallow circuits [13].

TensorFlow Quantum. TFQ [2] represents circuits (built with Cirq) as tensors and exposes simulation, expectation values and their gradients as Keras layers on top of TensorFlow [1]. The same model code can use exact, noisy or sampled simulation. Its tutorials include a version of the Farhi–Neven MNIST task, which we follow for the data pipeline. Huang et al. [5] discuss when classical models can match quantum ones on data like this, which is relevant to our baseline.

3 Setup

Data. We keep the 3s and 6s from MNIST [8] and downsample each image to $4\times4$ by bilinear resizing. As in the TFQ tutorial, we then remove training images whose downsampled form occurs with both labels, and binarise every pixel at a threshold of 0.5. Each lit pixel becomes an $X$ gate, so an image is the basis state $\ket{x}$ of 16 data qubits. Each seed draws 3000 training and 500 test images at random.

Models. QNN. A readout qubit is prepared with $X$ then $H$. Every data qubit $i$ then couples to it through $XX^{\theta_i}$, then $ZZ^{\phi_i}$, and a final $H$ is applied to the readout (Fig. 1). The prediction is $\langle Z\rangle\in[-1,1]$ on the readout, giving 32 parameters on 17 qubits. Hybrid. The same circuit, but $\langle Z\rangle$ on all 17 qubits feeds a dense layer with 8 ReLU units and a $\tanh$ output (185 parameters). Classical. A multilayer perceptron on the same 16 binary pixels, with 2 ReLU units and a $\tanh$ output (37 parameters), chosen to roughly match the QNN's parameter count.

Training. All models minimise the hinge loss on labels $\pm1$ with Adam [7] (learning rate 0.01, batch size 32) for 4 epochs, over 3 seeds. Quantum models are simulated exactly and differentiated with the adjoint method. We report mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds. We chose the batch size and learning rate from two candidate settings on seed 0.

Barren plateaus. Following McClean et al. [9], each random circuit applies $R_y(\pi/4)$ to every qubit, then layers of a uniformly random choice of $R_x$, $R_y$ or $R_z$ per qubit followed by a CZ ladder. We estimate $\mathrm{Var}[\partial\langle Z_0Z_1\rangle/\partial\theta_0]$ over 200 random circuits and angles, sweeping 2–12 qubits at 50 layers, and 1–50 layers at 8 qubits.

Noise. A single-qubit depolarizing channel with probability $p$ follows every moment of the model circuit. Expectations average 50 quantum trajectories. We run two measurements. (i) The $4\times4$ QNNs trained above are tested under noise on 500 test images (a separate random draw from the one in Table 1, so the noise-free accuracies differ slightly). (ii) Models are trained and tested with the noise present. Training through trajectories needs parameter-shift gradients, which made 17 qubits too slow, so (ii) uses $3\times3$ images (10 qubits), 200 training and 200 test images, 3 epochs, batch size 16 and learning rate 0.02.

Software and hardware. TensorFlow 2.18.1, TensorFlow Quantum 0.7.6 and Cirq 1.5.0, in a Linux Docker container with 8 CPU cores on an Intel laptop, with no GPU.

4 Results

4.1 The QNN learns the task, and a classical network of similar size does slightly better

The QNN reaches 0.868 $\pm$ 0.023 test accuracy (Table 1, Fig. 2). The classical network, with 37 parameters against the QNN's 32, reaches 0.909 $\pm$ 0.006. Both are bounded by the input: binarising to $4\times4$ leaves 193 distinct training patterns, and predicting each pattern's majority label scores 0.916 on the full test set. The classical model sits almost on that ceiling, so on this input there is little room for any model to do better. The QNN also took about 543 s to train in simulation, against 5 s for the classical network.

4.2 A classical head on the readouts closes the gap

Measuring $\langle Z\rangle$ on all 17 qubits and passing the values through an 8-unit dense layer raises accuracy by 3.8 points, to 0.906 $\pm$ 0.006 (Fig. 3). This matches the classical model. The gain cannot be attributed to the circuit alone: the hybrid model has 185 parameters, most of them classical, and 16 of its 17 readouts are on data qubits, which start in the input's basis state.

4.3 Gradients shrink as random circuits grow

At 50 layers, the gradient variance falls from $8.6\times10^{-2}$ at 2 qubits to $3.2\times10^{-3}$ at 8 qubits, a factor of 27 (Fig. 4, left). Beyond 8 qubits it stops falling in our sweep, reaching $2.8\times10^{-3}$ at 12 qubits, so a single exponential (decay factor 0.71 per qubit) describes the trend only loosely. The depth sweep suggests why. At 8 qubits the variance is still falling at 50 layers, 38 times below its single-layer value (Fig. 4, right), so 50 layers are probably not enough for the wider circuits to behave as random as the theory assumes [9]. We did not test this directly.

4.4 Depolarizing noise erodes accuracy quickly

The $4\times4$ QNNs from §4.1, tested under noise, go from 0.859 without noise to 0.770 at $p=0.01$ and 0.501 at $p=0.03$, which is chance (Fig. 5, Table 2). The channel acts after every moment of a circuit with 32 two-qubit couplings, so errors accumulate before the readout is measured. The $3\times3$ models trained with the noise present keep 0.859 at $p=0.01$, against 0.862 without noise, and drop to 0.753 at $p=0.03$. This comparison is confounded: the $3\times3$ circuit has 18 couplings rather than 32, so it accumulates less noise whether or not it is trained with it. Separating the effect of noise-aware training from circuit size would need noisy training at $4\times4$, which was too slow for us (§3).

5 Limitations

Everything here is classical simulation of small circuits. The task (two digits at $4\times4$ binary pixels) is far from where a quantum advantage could appear, and the input is so coarse that even a lookup table over training patterns sets a ceiling well below standard MNIST accuracies. We ran 3 seeds and lightly tuned two hyperparameters on one seed, so small differences between models should not be over-read. The noisy-training study uses smaller $3\times3$ images than the rest of the paper, and every noisy expectation carries trajectory-sampling error on top of the channel itself. The barren-plateau sweep stops at 12 qubits, with 200 circuits per point. None of these results are new. They reproduce known behaviour with current open-source tools.

6 Conclusion

TensorFlow Quantum makes the full loop (encoding classical data as circuits, training a QNN as a Keras layer, switching to noisy simulation, and probing gradient statistics) a few hundred lines of Python that run on a laptop CPU. On this small task, a classical model of similar size matches or beats the QNN, a classical head on the circuit's readouts brings the QNN up to that baseline, gradient variance shrinks as random circuits grow, and depolarizing noise quickly erodes a trained model's accuracy. These are the behaviours a newcomer to quantum machine learning should expect to see.

Reproducibility. All code, configurations and raw results ship with this paper. One command (python -m quantumtf_demo.run_all --preset full) reruns every experiment in Docker. Every number in the text is generated from the result files by a script, and sources.md records where each value comes from.
